# CBC / FHA / 11A / ADA / Section 504 — Key truths

This summary is meant to capture the core legal and code truths most relevant to CASp-style study and project review. It is not a substitute for the controlling code, statute, or official version of the rule in effect for a specific project. Always verify the adopted edition, jurisdiction, and funding source before keying an answer.

## 1. Pick the governing standard before answering a technical question

- California Building Code (CBC) Chapter 11B governs accessibility requirements for many public accommodations, commercial facilities, and public housing-type accessibility obligations in California.
- CBC Chapter 11A governs accessibility for private housing and associated dwelling-unit/common-area requirements in California.
- ADA Title III applies to public accommodations and commercial facilities.
- ADA Title II applies to state and local government programs, services, and facilities.
- Section 504 applies to federally funded programs and facilities.
- FHA (Fair Housing Act) applies to multifamily housing subject to federal housing accessibility requirements and is often considered alongside CBC Chapter 11A and applicable local requirements.
- The same project can implicate more than one standard. The question is not simply “which law is bigger,” but which law governs the project type, occupancy, funding, and authority.

## 2. CBC Chapter 11B is both a scoping code and a technical code

- Chapter 11B includes both applicability/scoping provisions and technical requirements.
- The analysis typically starts with: facility type, work scope, occupancy/use, authority, and code edition.
- The technical side includes accessible routes, parking, entrances, doors, toilet rooms, drinking fountains, signage, elevators, and other elements.
- A project may be required to comply with new construction standards, alterations, additions, or path-of-travel obligations depending on the facts.
- “Alteration” and “path of travel” questions are separate from the general question of whether the building is required to be accessible in the first place.

## 3. Accessible route and site circulation are always project-specific

- An accessible route must connect accessible parking, site arrival points, entrances, and usable destination areas in a continuous, usable way.
- The route must be evaluated in context: grade, crossings, curb ramps, entrances, transitions, and conflicts with vehicle circulation.
- Site design questions often hinge on the distinction between general circulation and the specific accessible route that is required by the project scope.
- The route requirement does not automatically mean the entire site or every path must be upgraded on a given project; the controlling scope and exception rules matter.

## 4. Parking is a separate accessibility topic from EV charging

- Accessible parking counts and van-accessible parking counts are determined by the governing table and the project type.
- Accessible parking is not the same as EV charging. EV charging spaces may be required in some projects, but they are not automatically counted as accessible parking spaces.
- Accessible parking layouts include dimensions, access aisles, markings, signage, and vertical clearance requirements that must be checked against the adopted code edition.
- Van-accessible layouts and stall/aisle configurations are a specific technical topic and must be verified against the controlling provision rather than memorized from a single example.

## 5. Doors, thresholds, and clear openings are not just “rough opening” math

- CBC accessibility requirements for doors and doorways focus on clear opening width, maneuvering clearances, operating force, hardware reach ranges, and threshold conditions.
- A rough opening is not the same as the required clear opening. The code is concerned with the actual usable opening and the accessibility of operation.
- Threshold, turning, and approach conditions can control whether an element is compliant even when the nominal width appears acceptable.

## 6. Plumbing and toilet-room requirements are technical and often scoping-sensitive

- Accessible toilet rooms, stalls, lavatories, sinks, and fixtures each have specific clearance and mounting requirements.
- The code distinguishes between required accessible fixtures, dimensions, and the quantity of fixtures that may be required based on occupancy and use.
- Fixture counts and accessibility requirements should not be conflated; each has its own calculation and triggering rule.

## 7. Housing standards are distinct from public accommodation standards

- CBC Chapter 11A and FHA rules govern housing accessibility in different but overlapping ways.
- Private multifamily housing may be governed by federal housing rules and California housing accessibility requirements, while public housing and some other facilities may fall under Chapter 11B or other standards.
- The key is to identify the housing type, the public/private classification, and the jurisdictional authority before applying a rule.
- Housing questions often require comparing private residential standards with public-accommodation standards; they are not interchangeable.

## 8. ADA and Section 504 are not “one-size-fits-all” substitutes for the CBC

- ADA requirements often overlap with CBC Chapter 11B in public accommodation and commercial settings, but they are not identical.
- Section 504 is triggered by federal funding and applies in a specific regulatory context.
- If a project is federally funded, the funding source and agency rules can create additional obligations beyond ordinary state code compliance.
- Code compliance must be tied to the project’s legal authority, not just the most familiar standard.

## 9. The practical CASp truth: identify authority, then apply the right standard

For CASp exam work and field review, the most reliable method is:

1. Identify the facility type and occupancy/use.
2. Determine whether the work is new construction, alteration, repair, or addition.
3. Identify the governing authority: CBC, ADA, FHA, Section 504, or another applicable standard.
4. Confirm the adopted code edition and effective date.
5. Apply the applicable scoping requirement before checking technical dimensions.
6. Document the reason the selected standard applies.

## 10. Common “don’t assume” rules

- Do not assume all accessible requirements are the same across CBC, ADA, FHA, and Section 504.
- Do not assume a project is governed by only one standard if multiple authorities apply.
- Do not assume EV charging spaces count as accessible parking spaces.
- Do not assume a rough opening is the same as a clear opening.
- Do not assume a percentage-based requirement can be used without checking the exact table and section.
- Do not use memorized numeric values without confirming the edition and context.

## 11. High-value study categories from the code framework

- Chapter 11B scoping and applicability
- Accessible routes and site circulation
- Accessible parking and van-accessible parking
- Doors, entrances, and clear width requirements
- Plumbing/toilet-room accessibility
- Housing comparison: Chapter 11A vs FHA vs public accommodation standards
- ADA/Section 504 overlap and funding-triggered obligations
- Standards identification scenarios: choosing the right rule set for the facts

## 12. Decision note for question writing

When creating test questions, the safest approach is to require the learner to identify the controlling authority, the relevant code section, and the project fact pattern before selecting the technical answer. This reduces the risk of treating overlapping standards as interchangeable.
